AI is re-weighting school subjects, not replacing them.

Across all 14 statutory subjects, AI is taking over specific outputs: routine prose, everyday translation, junior coding, stock illustration and background music. The same evidence makes checking, judgement, physical practice and people skills more valuable. Subjects built on producing text, code or images face the most change. Subjects built on bodies and relationships face the least.

AI and learning

How an AI tool is designed decides whether pupils gain or lose

The strongest study so far gave about 1,000 Turkish secondary students access to GPT-4 for maths practice, then took it away for the exam.

Change in score versus a control group with no AI. Source: Bastani et al., PNAS 2025. Field experiment, about 1,000 students.

The lesson for schools: "AI can do it" is a reason to keep teaching a skill and to assess it without AI. The guard-railed tutor avoided harm, but it did not beat the control group on the exam either.

Jobs evidence

Exposure is broad. Actual job loss so far is narrow, and it falls on junior roles.

Exposure studies estimate what AI could touch. They are not job-loss forecasts. The DfE's own study says it "does not distinguish whether a job will be augmented or replaced".

What has actually been measured

Future skills

The safest bets hold up in every scenario

No credible forecast covers specific school skills beyond 2035. So the sound approach is to invest in skills that stay valuable whichever future arrives.

Literacy and numeracy used to check AI

The foundation for spotting AI errors. 21% of adults in England are at or below Level 1 numeracy.

Evaluating evidence and sources

Lateral reading improved pupils' credibility judgements in six lessons. Analytical thinking is the WEF's top skill.

Interpersonal and civic skills

The strongest long-run evidence of rising value (Deming 2017). Customer-facing roles held up in UK data.

Physical competence

Least exposed to generative AI in every study. Robotics is the open question at 20–30 years.

Using AI without dependence

The CAR wants pupils to use AI "without becoming dependent on it". DfE now asks tools to monitor cognitive offloading.

Evidence tags, and what to check before quoting

EMPMeasured real outcomes: trials, payroll or job-posting data, official statistics
MODELEstimate of exposure or potential, or a projection. Never a measured job loss
SURVEYOpinions of employers, workers or experts
SPECScenario or informed speculation
VENDORProduced or funded by a firm selling AI or consulting
ADVOCACYFrom a body with an interest in the subject

Verify before relying on
  • UK graduate vacancies down 45.6% (Adzuna via a secondary source)
  • 2026 FrontierMath and SWE-bench scores from unofficial aggregators
  • Deezer's share of AI-generated uploads (company-reported)
  • The 32% illustrators figure (secondary source, sample unknown)
  • AllFaith religious-bias results (press coverage, partly preprint)
  • Exact wording of DfE's "cognitive offloading" standard
  • OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 (secondary summaries only)
  • Some programme-of-study wording (English, history, geography) not re-fetched
  • Whether the revised RSHE guidance names companion chatbots
Gaps in the evidence
  • No UK school trial of AI used directly by pupils has reported yet
  • No study tracks skills over more than about two years of AI use
  • No forecast covers any school subject's skills at 20 or 30 years
  • No UK payroll-data equivalent of the Stanford analysis

Scope: the national curriculum applies to England. Scotland (Curriculum for Excellence), Wales (Curriculum for Wales) and Northern Ireland have their own curricula and mostly treat AI as a cross-curricular safeguarding and digital-competence issue.
